Leonida’s first attraction does not have to be a robbery. In a Sunday opinion piece, Polygon argues that wandering, meeting residents and trying optional activities can be more appealing than rushing through GTA 6’s central story. This is an account of what the writer hopes to enjoy, rather than a review of the finished game ahead of its November 19 release.

Which side activities does Polygon highlight?

The outlet draws on the Netflix Extended Look and previews to list fishing, jet skiing, mini-golf, scuba diving, weightlifting, hunting, racing, fan boating, billiards, basketball and parachuting. Those examples come from Polygon’s account of previously shown material. The piece is not presenting a new, exhaustive activity checklist issued by Rockstar.

It also singles out Macca and Friends, an animated television show within the game. Our earlier article covered the Weazel programme. In this context, it represents another possible diversion: stopping to watch fictional television instead of immediately following the next mission marker. How rewarding that pause feels will depend on the finished experience.

Can ordinary encounters become the main attraction?

Much of the argument rests on memories of Red Dead Redemption 2. The writer recalls residents reacting to trouble and unplanned incidents becoming more memorable than portions of the campaign. These are experiences from Rockstar’s earlier game, used to explain an expectation for GTA 6. They are not evidence that identical interactions have been tested in Leonida.

Polygon also points back to a New York Times interview with Rob Nelson about bringing interactive detail from a sparsely populated historical setting into a dense modern environment. The outlet sees considerable promise in that shift. It does not establish how varied every pedestrian’s responses will be, or provide a measured assessment of the new systems.

Why it matters

The piece asks a different launch-week question: how much time will players want to spend away from the campaign? Its writer imagines virtual tourism through Art Deco streets and swamps. That perspective sits alongside our coverage of birdwatching and robbery preparation, rather than replacing the story with a single preferred way to play.

Our release page gathers practical information for getting started. Once inside the game, choosing a walk over a mission is a personal decision. The eventual depth, restrictions and progression links of these activities still need to be judged through play. An enthusiastic opinion column explains their appeal without settling those questions for the final release.
